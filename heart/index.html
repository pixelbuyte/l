03

What is modelled, and what is drawn

The timing is the load-bearing part

Systole is 420 ms at 60 bpm, shrinking as the 0.55 power of the beat length; diastole is whatever is left. That single asymmetry produces most of what you can see. At 60 bpm the ventricle spends nearly six tenths of the beat filling; at 180 it spends a third. Filling time collapses far faster than the beat does, and the part of it that goes first is diastasis — the idle middle, which is pure slack.

Only one valve at a time

A ventricle's inlet and outlet are never open together, and twice a beat neither is — those two intervals are where pressure is built and shed at constant volume. The drawing takes the valves from the phase, the pressure curves are drawn to agree, and the test suite checks the agreement in the other direction at every millisecond of every rate it allows.

Filling is where the rate bites

Volume arriving in the ventricle approaches a ceiling on an exponential. Shorten the time the valve is open and you cut the top off that curve — which is why, holding everything else still, output here climbs to about 150 bpm and then falls again: stroke volume is giving way faster than rate is gaining. It is also why the atrial kick, worth a fifth of filling at rest, is worth far more when there was no time for the rest of it.

The drawing is a diagram, not a scan

The shapes are hand-drawn Bézier curves in a single plane. A real heart is not planar: the outflow tracts cross, the valve planes are oblique, and the right ventricle wraps around the left rather than sitting beside it. Everything here is arranged for a reader looking at one flat picture. Treat the anatomy as a schematic that is honest about topology and approximate about geometry.

Honest limits

  • The numbers are plausible, not clinical. Pressures, volumes and the ejection fraction come from a small set of textbook relations tuned so that a resting adult lands near 120/80, 70 mL, 60%. They are not fitted to data and nothing here should be used to judge anything about a person.
  • Peak output is too low. Hold the sliders at hard exercise and this heart moves about eight litres a minute; a real untrained adult manages fifteen, and a trained one more than twenty. The shape of the curve is right — a rise, a plateau, a fall — and the height is not.
  • Arterial pressure is a rough derivation. Mean pressure comes from output against a fixed resistance and the pulse from stroke volume against a fixed compliance. Both change constantly in a real body, so the numbers here drift from what a cuff would read, most visibly at exercise, where the pulse pressure should widen and instead narrows.
  • Nothing here responds to anything. A real heart is governed second by second by the nervous system, and rate, contractility and filling move together rather than one slider at a time. Here they are three independent knobs, which is exactly what a body will not give you.
  • Conduction is assumed perfect. Every beat is identical, on time, and arrives everywhere at once. Most of what makes cardiology difficult is the beat that does not.
